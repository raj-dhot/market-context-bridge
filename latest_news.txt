CONTENT: S&P 500 vs. other major indexes Although the S&P 500 is widely regarded as the best gauge of how the U.S. stock market is doing, there are several other indexes that can be useful for investors as well, including: - Dow Jones Industrial Average - Nasdaq Composite - Russell 2000 (small-cap index) S&P 500 vs. Dow Jones Industrial Average The Dow Jones Industrial Average is a price-weighted index, meaning that the companies with the highest stock prices have the most influence on the index, regardless of their valuations. The Dow Jones Industrial Average lists only 30 companies and excludes some of the largest stocks in the market, including Amazon, Alphabet, and Berkshire Hathaway. Because the Dow is price-weighted, Goldman Sachs (GS +2.52%), with a share price of over $1,050 in early July 2026, has about 9.5 times the influence on the Dow's performance as Walmart (NYSE:WMT), despite Goldman's market cap being less than one-third of Walmart's. For these reasons, the S&P 500 is generally regarded as a more reliable stock market indicator. S&P 500 versus the Nasdaq The obvious difference between the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite Index is that the latter's stocks must be listed exclusively on Nasdaq. The S&P 500 is a mix of both Nasdaq and New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) stocks. You may also notice that when tech stocks are underperforming, the Nasdaq Composite tends to lag the S&P 500 because it has a higher proportion of technology stocks than the broader market, making it a more tech-heavy index. Another key difference is that while the S&P 500 consists of large-cap stocks, the Nasdaq Composite includes all qualified stocks listed on Nasdaq, making it more diverse in terms of market caps represented. S&P 500 versus the Russell Indexes The Russell Indexes are designed to provide benchmarks for the entire stock market. The Russell 1000 is the closest comparison to the S&P 500.
